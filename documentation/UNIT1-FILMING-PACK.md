## u1-allylic (2 min)

**To camera, opening:** Almost every reaction in this unit makes a charged carbon or a radical. Whether that carbon is allylic decides if the charge stays put or spreads out, and spread-out charge is what makes a species stable.

**Shot 1, on screen:** An allyl cation with the pi pair sliding across

> A carbon is allylic when it is bonded to a carbon of a C=C. Look at the charged carbon, then look one bond over. If you find a double bond there, you are allylic.

**Shot 2, on screen:** Both contributors side by side

> The C=C electrons can slide toward the charge. Only electrons move. Every atom stays where it is, so the two drawings are the same molecule seen two ways, which is exactly what resonance means.

**Shot 3, on screen:** Where the charge is shared

> After the slide, the charge sits on the far end of the old C=C. The two end carbons share it. The middle carbon never carries it, because it keeps four bonds in every drawing.

**To camera, closing:** Allylic means next to a C=C. A charge there is shared by the two end carbons. You will use this in every lesson that follows.

## u1-kvt (3 min)

**To camera, opening:** A diene plus HBr can give a 1,2 and a 1,4 product from the same cation. Neither is a mistake. Which one you mostly get depends on what you ask of the reaction.

**Shot 1, on screen:** One start, two humps, two wells

> Which product forms fastest? Which product is the most stable? These are separate questions and can have different answers. Speed is the height of the hump the reaction must climb. Stability is how deep the finished product sits.

**Shot 2, on screen:** Cold: the low hump wins

> The kinetic product has the lowest hump, so it forms first. When the reaction is cold, products cannot climb back out, so whatever forms fastest is what you keep.

**Shot 3, on screen:** Warm: products re-sort to the deep well

> The thermodynamic product sits in the deepest well. When the reaction is warm and reversible, products can climb out, go back and re-sort. Given time, most of the material ends up in the deepest well.

**To camera, closing:** Cold and irreversible: the lower hump wins. Warm and reversible: the deeper well wins. Read speed from the hump and stability from the well, and never mix them up.

## u1-nbs (2 min)

**To camera, opening:** Until now Br added across a C=C. Here we want to keep the C=C and swap one allylic H for Br instead. The reagent for that job is NBS.

**Shot 1, on screen:** Trace Br2 held by NBS

> A radical chain breaks the weakest C-H bond first. The allylic C-H is the weakest, because the radical it leaves is spread over two carbons, the same sharing idea as the allylic cation.

**Shot 2, on screen:** The allylic H leaving

> NBS releases Br2 only a trace at a time. With so little Br2 around, it does not add across the C=C. It only has time to react at the radical, so the allylic H is what gets replaced.

**Shot 3, on screen:** Why the C=C survives

> Light and ROOR do the same job: they start the chain. Which one a question uses does not change the product.

**To camera, closing:** NBS plus an initiator: find the allylic H, swap it for Br, keep the C=C. Because the radical is shared, the double bond can shift, so check both ends.

## u1-da (3 min)

**To camera, opening:** Diels-Alder joins a diene and a dienophile into a six-membered ring in one step. The dienophile is the partner that supplies a C=C. It makes two carbon-carbon bonds at once.

**Shot 1, on screen:** Diene and dienophile approaching

> There is no cation and no radical. Both new sigma bonds form together while the pi bonds shift. That is why this lesson has no ladder of intermediates.

**Shot 2, on screen:** Two new sigma bonds at once

> Two new sigma bonds form at the diene's ends. The diene's middle single bond becomes the ring's one C=C. So the product is always a cyclohexene.

**Shot 3, on screen:** The cyclohexene that results

> The diene's HOMO meets the dienophile's LUMO, with the lobes at the ends in phase, so both ends can bond at once. Orbitals that match mean the reaction is allowed.

**To camera, closing:** Diene plus dienophile gives a cyclohexene. Two new sigma bonds, and one C=C left between the diene's old C2 and C3.

## u1-dienes (2 min)

**To camera, opening:** In Diels-Alder both ends of the diene bond to the dienophile at the same time. That only works if the two ends can reach the same side.

**Shot 1, on screen:** s-cis against s-trans

> Rotate about the middle single bond. In s-cis the two C=C bonds sit on the same side, with the ends close together. In s-trans they sit on opposite sides, with the ends far apart.

**Shot 2, on screen:** A locked s-cis diene reacting

> Locked s-cis is fastest. A diene that can rotate is fine. Strained s-cis is slow. Locked s-trans does not react at all, because its ends can never reach the dienophile.

**Shot 3, on screen:** A locked s-trans diene that cannot

> Donor groups on the diene speed the reaction, but only if the diene can reach s-cis first. A diene stuck in s-trans does not react however good its donors are.

**To camera, closing:** Shape first, donors second. If the diene cannot reach s-cis, stop there.

## u1-dienophiles (2 min)

**To camera, opening:** The diene supplies electrons from its HOMO. The dienophile has to accept them into its LUMO. So the best dienophile is the one with the most welcoming LUMO.

**Shot 1, on screen:** A withdrawing group lowering the LUMO

> A group such as C=O, CN or NO2 pulls electron density away and lowers the dienophile's LUMO. A lower LUMO sits closer in energy to the diene's HOMO, so the reaction speeds up.

**Shot 2, on screen:** A donor slowing the reaction

> A donor group does the opposite: it raises the LUMO and the reaction slows down. This is the mirror of what donors do on the diene.

**Shot 3, on screen:** Where the groups land in the product

> The dienophile's groups end up on the sp3 carbons of the new ring, across the ring from the C=C. The two carbons the dienophile supplied become sp3.

**To camera, closing:** A fast dienophile has a withdrawing group. In the product, its groups sit on the sp3 carbons.

## u1-da-stereo (3 min)

**To camera, opening:** You can already draw the ring. Now ask which way the groups point. Because the reaction happens in one step with both partners meeting face to face, the answer is predictable, not random.

**Shot 1, on screen:** Cis stays cis

> Both new bonds form on the same face of the dienophile at the same moment. So groups that were cis on the dienophile are cis in the product, and trans stays trans.

**Shot 2, on screen:** Endo against exo

> The dienophile can approach with its withdrawing groups tucked under the diene (endo) or pointing away (exo). Under kinetic control the tucked-under approach is faster, so endo is the usual product.

**Shot 3, on screen:** Bridged and fused products

> A cyclic diene gives a bridged bicycle. A cyclic dienophile gives a fused one. Look at which partner carries the ring.

**To camera, closing:** Keep cis as cis, choose endo, then check bridged against fused. Every stereochemistry question in this unit comes from those three.
